Paradise for Sale: How Travel Ads Persuade You
Six techniques that sell a trip, and three questions that help you judge them.

Every travel ad sells the same product: a better version of you. A couple in white walks along an empty beach at golden hour, with no crowds and not a single cloud. The ad wants you to imagine yourself in that picture, and it wants you to book before the feeling fades. To do this, marketers use a small set of techniques, and once you can name them, you’ll be able to judge any ad for yourself.

The first technique is the perfect picture. Photographers often shoot beaches at sunrise, when the light is soft and the sand is still empty, and drones film the bay from above, so the water looks deep blue. Then editors crop the frame, so the parking lot, the construction site next door and the four-hour transfer from the airport stay outside it. The photo isn’t false; it’s simply chosen.

The second technique is emotional language. Ads love adjectives like unspoiled, breathtaking and secluded, which describe feelings more than facts, so nobody can prove them wrong. Their favorite phrase of all is “hidden gem.” A place isn’t really hidden if everyone knows about it, yet thousands of places are sold as hidden gems. Slogans work in the same way: “Your paradise is waiting” doesn’t tell you anything about the hotel, but it’s easy to remember.

Numbers are the third technique, because they sound scientific. “Over 300 days of sunshine a year” looks like a measurement, but the ad doesn’t usually say who counted the days, where or how.

The fourth and fifth techniques push you to decide quickly. Scarcity messages such as “Only 2 rooms left!” or “Exclusive offer: ends tonight!” make an ordinary weekend feel like a once-in-a-lifetime chance. Social proof does the rest: “14 people are looking at this hotel right now.” In his 1984 book Influence, the psychologist Robert Cialdini described both effects: we want things more when they seem rare, and we copy what other people do. These messages became so common that in 2019 the UK’s competition authority got six large booking sites to promise to change them.

The sixth technique is the famous face. When a well-known athlete smiles by a hotel pool, the hotel borrows some of the trust that fans feel for that person. Marketers choose a face that their target audience already admires, and they hope you’ll follow that person’s example.

That doesn’t mean the places are fake. The bay is real, and the sunset may truly be breathtaking. But smart travelers study ads the way engineers study data. Before you click “Book now,” I’d encourage you to ask three questions. What does this ad want me to feel? What did the camera leave out? And who benefits if I decide tonight? Paradise may be for sale, but you’re still free to choose what you pay for.
